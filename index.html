🎯 Case title: THE CHURCH USHER
1
🗣️
The Arrival
A 34-year-old church usher is brought to the emergency department on a Sunday afternoon. Several congregation members arrive with him. One elderly woman keeps saying: “Doctor, this can't be happening — he's the healthiest person I know.” His wife agrees: “He never misses church. He exercises. He doesn't smoke. He doesn't drink. He's never been admitted before.” Yet today he appears confused. When staff ask simple questions, his answers don't make sense. What is your opening differential?
2
👥
The History Begins
His wife provides more detail. Over the past few months she noticed small changes. Nothing dramatic. “Sometimes he would suddenly become irritable — then ten minutes later, completely normal.” “A few times he complained of dizziness.” “We thought he was just tired.” The episodes always passed. Nobody was particularly worried. Does this change your differential?
3
☕
The Church Elder
One of the church elders steps forward. “Doctor, there is something else. Last month during a meeting he became confused. He started speaking strangely. We thought he was joking. Then he asked where he was. Five minutes later he was completely normal.” No investigations were done. Life continued. What is your differential now?
4
🍪
The Small Detail
His wife suddenly remembers something. “Actually — he's been eating all the time.” “He carries biscuits everywhere.” “He keeps snacks in his car.” “If he doesn't eat regularly he feels awful.” “We used to tease him about it.” This detail is critical. What does it suggest?
5
🔬
The Blood Result
Blood results arrive. The nurse hands over the report. The emergency physician looks surprised. Blood glucose: 1.8 mmol/L — severe hypoglycaemia. Does this confirm or complicate your diagnosis?
6
💊
The Twist
A junior doctor asks: “Has he been taking any diabetic medication?” His wife answers: “No. He has never been diagnosed with diabetes.” No insulin. No diabetic tablets. No known endocrine history. Nothing. Now — what is your final diagnosis?
300
Discussion — 5 mins
✚ Diagnosis
INSULINOMA — a pancreatic tumour producing insulin endogenously, causing episodic hypoglycaemia in a non-diabetic patient.
💬 Key Insight The snack-carrying behaviour (Clue 4) is the tell — it represents the patient's unconscious self-treatment. Classic Whipple's Triad: symptomatic hypoglycaemia, glucose below 2.5 mmol/L, relief with glucose administration.
💡 Clinical Note Insulinoma is rare (1–4 per million per year) but is the most common functional pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour. 90% are benign and solitary. Surgical resection is curative in most cases. The diagnosis is often delayed by months or years because episodes are intermittent and self-resolving.
